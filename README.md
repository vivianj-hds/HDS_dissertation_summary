# Modelling the Effects of Climate Change on Community Mental Health Care Service Use in Australia
**Master's Dissertation Project Summary - UNSW 2026**

### Research Aims

To investigate climate change associations with community mental health care service use (CMHSU). 
CMHSU in this paper is defined as the number of mental health-related primary and secondary (i.e. specialists like psychiatry) 
services provided to outpatients in Australia. Specifically, this project investigates:

1. Associations between rising ambient temperature and CMHSU 
2. Associations between extreme climate events (high heat days, heat waves, drought and 
bushfires) and CMHSU 
3. Associations between climate events and total CMHSU during the COVID-19 
emergency period 
4. Associations between climate events and Telehealth CMHSU during the COVID-19 
emergency period 
5. Lagged effects of climate change events on CMHSU

### Methods

**Data Sources**

1. *Medicare Benefits Schedule*: for data on mental health-related primary and secondary services covered by Medicare
2. *Australian Climate Observations Reference Network - Surface Air Temperature* (ACORN-SAT): for weather station records suitable for climate change detection
3. *Emergency Events Database* (EM-DAT): for historical data on Australian disasters

**Statistical Methods**

For the core analyses, a time-series regression was conducted separately for each state/territory in Australia. 
This involved fitting a quasi-poisson regression model to Medicare data, accounting for seasonality and long-term trends using Fourier sine/cosine pairs and a linear function respectively. 
Using this as a baseline, the predictor variables were modelled and analysed for statistical significance. 

### Key Findings
1. Climate disasters (wildfires and drought) predicted significant decreases in mental health service use following exposure in some areas of Australia. These effects were found to be persistent, with lagged models showing reductions in service use months after the disaster. 
2. Little to no significant association was found between rising ambient temperatures/high heat days and mental health service use. 
3. During the COVID-19 Emergency period, these effects were no longer significant.
